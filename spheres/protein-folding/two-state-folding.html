ΔG = ΔH − TΔS · Two-State Folding

Proteins are only marginally stable, often 5–15 kcal/mol, the small difference of two huge terms ΔH and TΔS. With a heat-capacity change ΔC_p the stability curve bends down at both ends: heat and cold denaturation. Two-state kinetics give the chevron plot.

LIT What the VM computes here: The VM evaluates ΔG(T) at 27 temperatures, using its own ln series (atanh form) and exp. It finds the cold-denaturation temperature by 50 chained bisection steps and builds the 17-point chevron. JS only plots.

LIT Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. At the melting temperature ΔG(T_m) = 0, so ΔS_m = ΔH_m/T_m. With a constant ΔC_p: ΔG(T) = ΔH_m(1 − T/T_m) − ΔC_p[(T_m − T) + T ln(T/T_m)], the Gibbs–Helmholtz stability curve (Becktel & Schellman 1987).

LIT Two-state folding (U ⇌ N, no intermediates): K = e^(−ΔG/RT), and the folded fraction is f = 1/(1 + e^(−ΔG_unfold/RT)). Chymotrypsin inhibitor 2 was the first protein shown to fold this way (Jackson & Fersht 1991).

LIT Kinetics: k_obs = k_f + k_u. Each rate depends exponentially on denaturant (ln k_f = ln k_f⁰ − m_f[D]/RT, ln k_u = ln k_u⁰ + m_u[D]/RT), which gives the V-shaped chevron. ΔG(H₂O) = RT ln(k_f⁰/k_u⁰).

AMBER The typical range of 5–15 kcal/mol is a textbook generalisation.
