Levinthal’s Paradox · 3²⁰⁰ vs a Millisecond

A 100-residue chain has 200 backbone torsions. At 3 states each that is 3²⁰⁰ ≈ 2.7 × 10⁹⁵ shapes, so a blind search would outlast the universe by 10⁶⁵, yet small proteins fold in milliseconds or less.

LIT What the VM computes here: The VM computes 3²⁰⁰ by recursive powers (its call depth is capped at 48, so the power is split into blocks of 32). It does all the time conversions, and it runs the whole Zwanzig-style first-passage sum, unrolled into up to 600 statements. JS only draws the chart.

LIT Cyrus Levinthal (1969, “How to fold graciously”) pointed out that an unfolded chain has an astronomical number of conformations. A common way to count: 100 residues → 200 φ/ψ torsions → 3 stable states each → 3²⁰⁰ ≈ 10⁹⁵.

LIT Even sampling one conformation every 0.1 ps (10¹³ per second), exhaustive search needs about 10⁷⁵ years, versus 1.38 × 10¹⁰ years for the age of the universe. Real small proteins fold in µs to s. That mismatch is the paradox.

LIT Zwanzig, Szabo and Bagchi (PNAS 1992) showed that a small, physically reasonable energy bias against locally wrong configurations, of the order of a few kT, cuts Levinthal’s time to a biologically significant size. Folding is a biased search, not a random one.

AMBER Levinthal’s own estimate is often quoted as 10³⁰⁰; the 3²⁰⁰ count is the textbook simplification. Both are about the size of the search, not real folding.

WALL The paradox does not say folding is impossible. It says folding cannot be an unbiased random search. How the bias is encoded in each sequence is still not fully solved.

MODEL Biased search: a birth–death chain on the number of wrong bonds after Zwanzig et al. Each wrong bond is fixed at 10⁹/s, and each right bond breaks to one of ν = 2 wrong states at ν·10⁹·e^(−U)/s. The I-13 VM computes the exact mean first-passage time from all-wrong to all-right. At U = 0 the result equals the blind-search scale; at a few kT it drops toward milliseconds.
